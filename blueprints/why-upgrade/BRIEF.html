Measured from the source tree: the 17.x line carries 545 release-note items across 11 minors, 18.x carries 351 across 5, and 55 distinct CVEs appear in 17.x alone. Nobody reads 496 entries — so nobody reads any of them, and the handful that matter get missed. The reduction is the product.

The break-glass example

CVE-2026-6471 · shipped in 17.11 and 18.6

A new output_plugin_libraries GUC whitelists logical-decoding plugins, defaulting to pgoutput, test_decoding. Anyone running wal2json or decoderbufs finds logical replication broken after a patch-level upgrade until postgresql.conf is edited. pg_upgrade --check fails too.

It is also detectable in one query against pg_replication_slots. That gap — between a production break and a query nobody thought to run — is the whole thesis.

The finding that justifies it on its own

Upstream's own migration chain is lossy

Each release's migration section points back to one earlier release: 17.11 → “see 17.6”, 17.6 → “see 17.5”. So a user upgrading 17.4 → 17.11 who follows the official documentation correctly lands on 17.6 and 17.11 — and silently misses 17.5 entirely, including its self-referential-FK corruption item and its BRIN bloom data-loss item.

Following the docs properly still loses data-corruption remediation steps. Nothing else here is a stronger argument for building this.

The core mechanic — exposure, not “matching score”

Stage 1, capability gate: profile the database once — logical replication? BRIN? which extensions? A fix declares what it requires; no logical replication means every logical-decoding fix collapses instantly. Stage 2, trigger check: you use the subsystem, but do your circumstances actually fire this bug? Sometimes trivial, sometimes impossible. Saying which is the product's integrity.

On the name: “matching” reads as search relevance, and “score” implies a number — a bare 0.73 is false precision in a safety-adjacent judgment. Operators already have the term of art from the CVE world: are we exposed? So: an ordinal ladder with evidence, never a naked float.

LevelMeaningExample
CONFIRMEDProven from the catalog, evidence attached3 slots on wal2json
LIKELYSubsystem in use, one precondition unprovable—
POSSIBLESubsystem in use, trigger not checkablerace under concurrent DDL

Two invariants, or the product is worse than useless

1. UNKNOWN is never folded into NOT_EXPOSED. They are opposite claims; conflating them is how someone gets paged at 3am.

2. The ladder is deliberately asymmetric. A false NOT_EXPOSED is far worse than a false POSSIBLE — so claim it only on positive proof of absence, never on absence of evidence. Severity stays orthogonal: exposure is likelihood, severity is impact, priority is the product of the two.

The data model insight

For CVE-2026-6471, the 17.11 and 18.6 release-note entries cite the identical master commit hashes (226e49cbe, 20e9dcefd). So the set of master commits is a stable canonical identity for a logical fix across every branch that received it — dedup is deterministic, not fuzzy text matching.

That gives two tables: fix (keyed by master commit, holding all enrichment) and fix_release (one row per back-patched branch). The interval logic then falls out with no special cases — including the case a naive implementation gets wrong: upgrading 17.11 → 18.6 must exclude CVE-2026-6471, because its 17-line fix is already held.

But a single interval is not enough. The corpus contains four distinct semantics, and getting this wrong is how you confidently suppress a real finding:

SemanticsConditionWhy it bites
transientran in [introduced, fixed)Upgrade and it's gone. Nothing to do.
residualever ran in that rangeUpgrading stops new damage, doesn't repair old. Depends on history, not on from.
creation-timecluster created under a bad versionCreated on 18.1, now on 18.6 → still broken forever without catalog surgery.
peer-versiontwo nodes at different versionsThe standard standbys-first rollout is the trigger window.

Never invent introduced_in. Backpatch-through: 14 says where the fix went, not where the bug started. Guessing produces confidently-wrong suppression — the same failure class as a false NOT_EXPOSED. Assert it only when a commit names the culprit; otherwise over-report and say so.

Two traps already in the wild: 18.5 was stamped and never released, yet postgresql.org's security pages still say “Fixed: 18.5” (they record the wrap number, not the shipped one) — and Patroni's metadata has copied it. Join on that string and you hand users a version they cannot install.

Three constraints that would otherwise bite

Managed services

Probe, don't infer

RDS backports onto frozen minors and lags community. “You're on 17.4 so you're vulnerable to 17.5–17.11” is wrong both ways. Ask the server what it can do — probe for output_plugin_libraries — never do version arithmetic.

Multi-database

Per-database blindness

pg_proc, pg_constraint, pg_extension are per-database. One connection reporting “not affected” is wrong on any multi-DB cluster — template0 included. Design in from day one.

Detection SQL

Curate, don't generate

A hand-rolled partition-FK check produced four false positives during research; upstream's shipped query was correct. Ship a regression-tested query library, never runtime-generated SQL.

And one category we're uniquely placed to own: “this upgrade will change your metrics.” Query IDs change at 18.2 for every GROUP BY query, breaking pg_stat_statements history; wait-event names changed in 17.3 and again in 17.6. Nothing is corrupted, so upstream assigns no severity — but dashboards and baselines silently break.

Strategic fit

This is the manifesto made executable. A managed provider schedules your window, hands you a version number, and tells you nothing about what is inside it — while your own pg_replication_slots already holds the answer. That asymmetry is not technical, it is enclosure. Access, full control, ownership — applied to upgrade decisions.

It is also proof of the “AI breaks the moat” thesis rather than merely an application of it: enriching ~900 fixes was historically a team of Postgres hackers. A pipeline with an expert review gate makes it possible at solo-founder scale. The product is the demonstration of the argument.

The honest tension

This deepens the “Postgres guy” frame that the wider strategy wants headroom beyond. The mitigation is architectural and cheap if decided now: the engine — upstream fix corpus × capability profile → ranked exposure — is not Postgres-specific. Build it generic; let Postgres be vertical #1.

Decisions taken

  1. 01

    Exposure ladder + evidence, severity orthogonal.

    Not a “matching score”. No naked floats.

  2. 02

    The unit of analysis is a fix, not a feature.

    Features belong with major upgrades, in v2, only if this earns it.

  3. 03

    KB authored platform-side, mirrored into the npm bundle at build time.

    Offline-first by default; follows the existing embed-checkup-dictionary precedent. ~15–90 KB on a 312 KB tarball.

  4. 04

    Two-person sign-off on every detector before publication.

    A false NOT_EXPOSED is the one failure this product cannot have.

  5. 05

    One-shot personalization stays free forever; fleet and audit artifacts are paid.

    Gate the magic and the growth loop dies.

  6. 06

    Credit the prior art loudly and contact its author before launch.

    In a community this small, doing it quietly is a self-inflicted wound.

Open questions

  • Q1

    Curation depth vs. breadth for v1.

    ~900 items. Deep enrichment on the ~150 action-required and security items with the rest as passthrough — or shallow across the whole corpus? Recommendation: the former.

  • Q2

    Do we open-source the curated knowledge base?

    Maximum citation, community goodwill, and on-message for the freedom frame — but it is the expensive asset. Recommendation: open the data, keep the personalization engine and fleet commercial.

  • Q3

    Managed services in v1 or v2?

    Largest install base and the most on-message segment, but vendor version strings do not map 1:1 and superuser is unavailable — the hardest place to be correct.

  • Q4

    Who owns release-day freshness, permanently?

    Quarterly waves plus out-of-cycle emergencies. A standing commitment, not a launch task.

  • Q5

    How much third-party extension coverage?

    Recommendation: a curated known-incidents list for the top ~10 plus ALTER EXTENSION UPDATE detection — not a full matrix.

  • Q6

    Do all three surfaces ship together, or CLI + public page first?

    They share the engine; the Console is the natural second wave.
